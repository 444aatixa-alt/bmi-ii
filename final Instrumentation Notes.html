What it is

Analyzes how matter interacts with electromagnetic radiation (light) by measuring absorption, emission, or scattering at different wavelengths.

Biomedical importance

Measures hemoglobin, glucose, electrolytes, proteins, and bilirubin; widely used in routine blood testing and clinical diagnostics.

What determines the result

The wavelength of light used, the concentration and chemical structure of the substance being measured, and Beer-Lambert Law: absorbance = ε × c × l

How the choices follow

Wavelength

The application decides the wavelength. The wavelength decides the light source (ultraviolet needs deuterium or xenon, visible needs tungsten) and the cuvette material (ultraviolet needs quartz).

Detector

How faint the light is decides the detector: normal light uses a photodiode, many wavelengths at once uses an array or charge-coupled device, very faint light uses a photomultiplier tube.

Optics

Lenses, mirrors and fibers are chosen to carry that wavelength without absorbing it, for example quartz lenses for ultraviolet and gold mirrors for infrared.

Temperature sensor

The temperature sensor is needed wherever the signal changes with temperature on its own: enzyme kinetics and fluorescence most of all.

1. Hemoglobin and biochemical assays

  • Hemoglobin is measured by absorption spectrophotometry.
  • Blood is mixed with a reagent that turns hemoglobin into a stable coloured compound (cyanmethemoglobin).
  • Light of about 540 nm is passed through the sample, and the absorbance is read.
  • By the Beer-Lambert law, absorbance is proportional to concentration, so the hemoglobin level is found by comparing with a standard.
  • Other biochemical tests (glucose, cholesterol, urea, creatinine) work the same way: a reagent produces a coloured product, and the colour intensity shows the amount.
  • This is used to diagnose anaemia and to monitor patients in clinical chemistry labs.

2. DNA and protein amount

  • DNA absorbs UV light at about 260 nm and protein at about 280 nm.
  • A small sample is placed in the spectrophotometer, and the absorbance is read at both wavelengths.
  • The concentration is calculated from the absorbance.
  • The ratio of 260/280 shows purity: a ratio of about 1.8 means pure DNA, and a lower value suggests protein contamination.
  • This is used in molecular biology labs to check samples before tests such as PCR or sequencing.

3. Enzyme kinetics

  • Enzyme activity is measured by following a reaction over time.
  • Many reactions use up or produce a substance that absorbs light, for example NADH, which absorbs at 340 nm.
  • The spectrophotometer records absorbance repeatedly, and the rate of change of absorbance is the reaction rate.
  • A faster change means higher enzyme activity.
  • This is used to measure enzymes in blood, such as liver enzymes (ALT, AST), to help diagnose disease, and to study how drugs or conditions affect enzymes.

4. Drug amount

  • Many drugs absorb UV or visible light at a known wavelength (paracetamol, for example, absorbs in the UV range).
  • The sample is dissolved, and its absorbance is measured.
  • The concentration is found by comparing with standard solutions of known strength.
  • This is used in quality control to check that tablets contain the correct dose, and in measuring drug levels in samples.

5. Fluorescence assays

  • The sample is lit with light of one wavelength (excitation).
  • It absorbs this and gives off light at a longer wavelength (emission).
  • A detector, placed at a right angle to the source so it does not pick up the excitation light, measures the emitted light.
  • The intensity is proportional to the amount of the substance.
  • Fluorescence is more sensitive than absorption, so it can detect very small amounts.
  • It is used with fluorescent dyes or labels in tests such as immunoassays, DNA detection and cell studies.

6. Infrared identification

  • Infrared light makes the chemical bonds in a molecule vibrate.
  • Each type of bond (O-H, C=O, N-H) absorbs at its own characteristic wavelength, so a sample produces a pattern of absorption peaks that works like a fingerprint.
  • The pattern is compared with known spectra to identify the substance.
  • Modern instruments use FTIR, which is fast and needs very little sample.
  • This is used to identify unknown substances, check drug purity, and detect fake or contaminated materials.
  • Unlike the other applications, it tells you what the substance is rather than how much there is.

What it is

An optical microscope lights up a specimen and magnifies it so you can see small details. In a digital microscope, an image sensor records the magnified image and turns it into electronic data.

Biomedical importance

Identifies bacteria, parasites, abnormal cells, and tissue pathology; critical for microbiology cultures, blood smears, and histopathology diagnosis.

What determines the result

Magnification level, resolution, specimen preparation method, staining techniques, and the type of microscopy used (light, electron, fluorescence).

  2. Condenser
  3. Specimen
  4. Objective lens
  5. Eyepiece / Camera
  6. Display

How the choices follow

Specimen and objective

The application decides how the specimen is prepared and lit (stain and bright-field, phase contrast, or fluorescence), and that decides the objective.

Detector

How faint the light is decides the detector: bright stained samples use a camera, very faint fluorescence uses a sensitive camera or a photomultiplier tube.

Sensors

Anything that moves or must stay steady gets a sensor: the stage and focus, the lamp, and living cells.

Confocal microscopy

In confocal microscopy a laser scans one point at a time, a pinhole blocks out-of-focus light, and a photodiode or photomultiplier tube detects the light from each point.

1. Blood-cell morphology

  • Specimen: Stained blood smear
  • Illumination and contrast: Bright-field, with Wright-Giemsa stain
  • Typical objective: 40×, then 100× oil
  • Detector: CMOS or CCD camera
  • Sensors that matter: Light sensor; position encoder when scanning
  • Result: Cell shape and size, abnormal cells
  • Flow: Blood smear → stain → Light source → Condenser → Specimen → Objective (100× oil) → Camera → Image / report

2. Histology and pathology

  • Specimen: Stained tissue section (hematoxylin and eosin)
  • Illumination and contrast: Bright-field
  • Why this method fits: Bright-field is used because the H&E stain already gives the tissue colour contrast, so no special optics are needed.
  • Typical objective: 4× to 40×
  • Detector: CMOS or CCD camera (slide scanner)
  • Sensors that matter: Position encoder and autofocus for slide scanning
  • Result: Diagnosis by a pathologist, digital slide
  • Flow: Tissue slice → H&E stain → Light source → Condenser → Specimen → Objective → Camera → Diagnosis

3. Microbiology

  • Specimen: Stained bacteria (for example Gram stain) or a wet mount
  • Illumination and contrast: Bright-field for stained bacteria; phase contrast for wet mounts
  • Why this method fits: Bright-field is used for stained bacteria because the stain gives contrast. Phase contrast is used for wet mounts because living bacteria are unstained and nearly transparent.
  • Typical objective: 100× oil
  • Detector: Camera
  • Sensors that matter: Light sensor
  • Result: Shape, arrangement, Gram type
  • Flow: Sample → smear + Gram stain → Light source → Condenser → Specimen → Objective (100× oil) → Camera → Bacteria type

4. Cell culture

  • Specimen: Living cells in flasks or plates
  • Illumination and contrast: Phase contrast, because unstained living cells are nearly transparent
  • Typical objective: 4× to 20×
  • Detector: Camera
  • Sensors that matter: Temperature sensor; position encoder for time-lapse
  • Result: Growth, health, how crowded the cells are
  • Flow: Dish in incubator → Light source (above) → Condenser + phase ring → Specimen → Objective (below dish) → Camera → Screen (temperature sensor keeps cells alive)

5. Fluorescence imaging

  • Specimen: Cells or tissue labeled with a fluorescent dye or antibody
  • Illumination and contrast: Fluorescence: excitation filter, then emission filter
  • Why this method fits: Fluorescence is used because the target molecule is labelled with a dye, and only the labelled structures glow against a dark background. This gives high specificity.
  • Typical objective: 20× to 100×. 20× is used to survey the field and 100× to see detail inside the cell.
  • Detector: Sensitive camera, or photodiode / PMT in confocal
  • Sensors that matter: Temperature sensor; autofocus
  • Result: Where a molecule is inside the cell
  • Flow: Labeled sample → Excitation light → Excitation filter → Dichroic mirror → Objective → Specimen glows → Emission filter → Camera / PMT → Software

6. Parasite identification

  • Specimen: Stained blood smear (for example for malaria)
  • Illumination and contrast: Bright-field
  • Why this method fits: Bright-field is used because the Giemsa stain colours the parasite and the red cells differently, so it stands out.
  • Typical objective: 100× oil
  • Detector: Camera
  • Sensors that matter: Autofocus and position encoder in automated scanners
  • Result: Parasite species and count
  • Flow: Blood / stool → stain → Light source → Condenser → Specimen → Objective → Camera → Parasite ID

7. Biomedical research

  • Specimen: Varies: live cells, tissue, organisms
  • Illumination and contrast: Chosen to match the specimen: fluorescence, confocal or live-cell imaging
  • Why this method fits: The method is chosen to match the specimen: fluorescence for labelled molecules, confocal for sharp optical sections in thick samples, and live-cell imaging for living cells over time. Low power is used to locate the area and high power for detail.
  • Typical objective: Varies
  • Detector: Camera, or photodiode / PMT
  • Sensors that matter: All of them: temperature, autofocus, position encoder, light sensor
  • Result: Measurable image data
  • Flow: Prepared sample → whichever chain above fits → Camera → Analysis software

What it is

A separation technique that divides a mixture into individual components based on their different affinities for a mobile and stationary phase.

Biomedical importance

Identifies and quantifies drugs, metabolites, and toxins in blood/urine; essential for therapeutic drug monitoring and detecting substance abuse.

What determines the result

The chemical properties of each component (polarity, molecular weight, charge), the stationary phase material, mobile phase composition, and flow rate.

What it is

A centrifuge is an instrument that separates (does not measure) the components of a mixture by spinning it at high speed. The spinning creates centrifugal force, which pushes denser particles outward to the bottom of the tube while lighter material stays on top.

Biomedical importance

Fundamental tool for understanding how cells work and for producing biological therapies.

  • Centrifugation separates biological materials by density using extreme rotational force, allowing researchers to isolate organelles, proteins, and DNA from cells much faster than gravity alone.
  • This capability is essential for studying cell structure, purifying proteins for medicine, diagnosing diseases (like separating blood components), and producing vaccines and therapeutics.
  • Without centrifugation, many modern biomedical advances—from understanding how cells work to developing treatments—would be impossible.

What determines the result

The rotor type (fixed-angle or swinging-bucket), the speed (given as rpm or × g, where × g is the relative centrifugal force), the time, and which layer you keep.

Rotating shaftA small magnet is fixed on the shaft. Each turn, it swings past a fixed point.
Hall sensorA chip that gives a small voltage pulse each time the magnet passes (the Hall effect). Faster spin = pulses closer together.
Pulse processingAn amplifier and comparator clean the pulses. A counter in the microcontroller counts them. RPM = (pulses per second ÷ pulses per turn) × 60. Example: 80 pulses in 1 second with 1 magnet = 4800 RPM.
Display and controlThe screen shows RPM. The controller compares measured and set speed, then changes the motor power (using pulse-width modulation) to correct it. This is a closed-loop control system.

Which chain to use

1. Process: motor → rotor → tubes → centrifugal force → separated layers

Use this when the point is separating something.

  • Blood sample prep: spin a tube to get plasma or serum on top and red cells at the bottom, before it goes into a chemistry analyzer.
  • Haematology: a haematocrit (PCV) test, where blood in a capillary tube is spun and the red cell fraction is read off.
  • Urine analysis: spin down urine so the sediment (cells, crystals, bacteria) can be looked at under a microscope.
  • Blood bank: separate donated blood into red cells, plasma and platelets.
  • Microbiology and lab work: pellet bacteria or cells from a liquid culture.

2. Feedback loop: controller → motor → Hall sensor → RPM → back to controller

Use this when the point is the speed has to be exact and stay constant.

  • Blood bank component separation: the speed must be precise so platelets and plasma separate properly without damaging the cells.
  • Clinical chemistry labs: every run must use the same speed so results are repeatable between samples and between days.
  • Cell culture and research: gentle, exact speeds so cells are pelleted without being crushed.
  • Ultracentrifuges (DNA, proteins, viruses): very high speeds where small errors change the result a lot, so the speed must be measured and corrected constantly.
  • Safety monitoring: the RPM reading is also what lets the machine detect imbalance or overspeed and shut down.

What it is

A chemistry analyzer is a machine that measures how much of a chemical substance is in a patient sample such as serum, plasma or urine. Automated analyzers combine fluid handling, controlled reaction conditions, optical or electrochemical measurement and software.

Biomedical importance

High-throughput testing of glucose, enzymes, lipids, kidney/liver function markers; enables rapid diagnosis and patient monitoring.

What determines the result

Sample quality, reagent quality/freshness, instrument calibration, temperature control, and the specific analytical method programmed into the machine.

Functional blocks: what the machine does

  1. 1. Sample IDbarcode read
  2. 2. Sample aspirationfew µL drawn
  3. 3. Reagent dispensingadds chemistry
  4. 4. Mix / incubateabout 37 °C
  5. 5. Measurelight or ions
  6. 6. Calculate / reportcalibration

Blocks 1 to 4 get the right reaction. Block 5 turns the reaction into a signal. Block 6 turns the signal into an answer.

Instrumentation chain: how color becomes a number

  1. Colored reaction 
  2. Photodiodelight → current
  3. Amplifierbigger, cleaner
  4. ADCvolts → number
  5. Concentrationmg/dL, mmol/L

This chain lives inside blocks 5 and 6. Electrolytes (Na+, K+, Cl−) skip the color step: an ion-selective electrode (ISE) makes a voltage directly.

A photodiode turns the light that gets through the colored liquid into a tiny current. An amplifier makes it a clean, larger voltage. The analog-to-digital converter (ADC) turns the voltage into a number. Software uses a calibration to turn that number into a concentration.

Sensor vs detector: the difference

Sensor

Checks the instrument: is the probe in the liquid, is the incubator at 37 °C, is the cuvette in place, which tube is this?

It keeps the machine working correctly.

Detector

Measures the sample result: how much color, or how many ions, the substance produced.

It gives the signal that becomes the answer.

Rule of thumb: if you removed it, would the machine lose its ability to measure the substance (detector), or only its ability to run safely and correctly (sensor)?

Why they matter

Correct volume → accurate concentration

A wrong volume shifts the final result by the same proportion.

Stable temperature → correct reaction rate

Enzyme reactions speed up or slow down with temperature.

Optical detector → measures color change

It is the only part here that measures the substance itself.

ISE → direct electrolyte measurement

Reads ions directly, with no color reaction.

Level/pressure sensing → reduces aspiration errors

Catches empty tubes, clots and air before they ruin a result.

Barcode → prevents sample mix-up

Keeps each result attached to the right patient.

How the tables connect

Test and wavelength

The test ordered decides the reagent and the wavelength. The wavelength decides which filter or grating setting the photometer uses.

Reading type

A reaction that changes color gives an endpoint reading, and an enzyme reaction gives a rate reading, which is why enzyme tests lean most on temperature control. Electrolytes skip the optics and go through the ISE unit.

Sensors

The sensors do not measure the sample. They keep volume, temperature and position correct so the detector reading converts to a trustworthy concentration.

Points to include in any answer

  • Name the method, the wavelength, the detector and the sensor for each. That is the "tied to the components" part, and it is what separates a good instrumentation answer from a general chemistry one.
  • Most tests share the same chain: sample + reagent, then light through the cuvette, then photodiode, then processor. You can say this once at the start and then only state what is different for each test.
  • Remember the odd ones out: liver enzymes are rate-based (slope), and electrolytes are non-optical (ISE).

1. Glucose

  • Glucose is measured by an enzyme reaction.
  • The enzyme acts on glucose and produces either a coloured product, read at about 500 nm, or a change in NADH, read at about 340 nm.
  • The light passes through the reaction cuvette and a photodiode measures how much is absorbed.
  • The absorbance is proportional to the glucose concentration.
  • Temperature sensors matter because the reaction rate depends on temperature, and liquid level sensors check that the correct volume of sample and reagent was taken.
  • It is used to diagnose and monitor diabetes.

2. Urea

  • Urea is measured by an enzyme reaction that consumes NADH.
  • NADH absorbs light at about 340 nm, so as urea is broken down, the absorbance falls.
  • The photodiode records this fall, and the rate of fall gives the urea concentration.
  • Because this is a rate measurement, the temperature must be controlled and monitored by a temperature sensor, otherwise the rate would change for the wrong reason.
  • It is used to assess kidney function.

3. Creatinine

  • Creatinine is measured either by the Jaffe colour reaction or by an enzymatic method.
  • The coloured product is read at about 500 to 550 nm with a photodiode, and the colour intensity gives the creatinine concentration.
  • The sample volumes are very small, so liquid level and pressure sensors are important to make sure the right amount is picked up and dispensed.
  • It is used to assess kidney function.

4. Liver enzymes (ALT, AST)

  • Here the analyzer measures enzyme activity, not a simple concentration.
  • The enzyme reaction uses up NADH, so the absorbance at about 340 nm falls over time.
  • The photodiode reads this repeatedly, and the slope of the fall gives the enzyme activity in U/L.
  • Temperature sensors are especially important because enzyme rate roughly doubles for every 10 °C rise, so a small temperature error gives a large result error.
  • It is used to detect liver damage and monitor liver disease.

5. Lipids (cholesterol, triglycerides)

  • Enzyme reactions convert the lipid into a coloured product.
  • The product is read at about 500 to 550 nm with a photodiode, and the colour intensity gives the lipid concentration.
  • Temperature sensors keep the reaction conditions steady, and liquid level sensors check the volumes.
  • It is used to assess the risk of heart disease and to monitor treatment.

6. Proteins (total protein, albumin)

  • Total protein is measured with the biuret reaction, which gives a coloured product read at about 540 nm.
  • Albumin is measured by dye binding, which changes colour and is read at about 600 nm.
  • The photodiode measures the absorbance, which gives the protein concentration.
  • Temperature sensors keep the reaction stable, and position sensors make sure the cuvettes and the sampling arm are in the correct place.
  • It is used to assess liver and kidney conditions and nutritional status.

7. Electrolytes (Na+, K+, Cl−)

  • This one is different because it is not optical.
  • Ion-selective electrodes (ISE) are used instead of light and a photodiode.
  • Each electrode responds to one ion and produces a voltage related to the concentration of that ion in the sample.
  • Temperature sensors matter because the electrode response depends on temperature, and liquid level sensors check the sample volume.
  • It is used to check fluid balance, kidney function and heart rhythm risks, since sodium and potassium levels directly affect them.

Endpoint vs rate (kinetic) tests

Analyzers read color in two different ways, depending on the test.

Endpoint test

The machine waits for the reaction to finish, then measures the final color once.

Example: glucose.

Rate (kinetic) test

The machine measures how fast the color changes over time.

Examples: enzymes such as ALT and AST. Enzyme activity is the speed of the reaction.

Vertical axis = absorbance (color). Left: one reading at the end. Right: readings at two times, and the slope between them is the result.

Both use the same photodiode. The difference is what the software does with the readings: take the final one, or work out how fast they change.

Electrolytes work a little differently

Sodium, potassium and chloride are usually measured with ion-selective electrodes (ISE). There is no color and no photodiode. The electrode produces a small voltage that depends on the ion concentration.

  1. Sample ionsin ISE module
  2. ISE electrodeion → voltage
  3. Amplifierbigger, cleaner
  4. ADCvolts → number
  5. Concentrationmmol/L

After the electrode, the chain is the same: amplifier → ADC → software → result.
